# Lesson 24 — Moment equations (fitting a mean *and* a variance)

**Feature:** treating statistical moments as ODE observables · **Difficulty:** ★★★

Every fit so far treated the model's ODE states as concentrations or amounts. But an
ODE state can also be a **statistical moment** of a stochastic process. For a class of
models the mean and variance obey their own closed ODEs, and you can fit those moment
trajectories directly — no stochastic simulation required — to recover the underlying
rate constants.

## The process

A linear **birth–death–immigration** process:

```
0 → X    immigration, rate s
X → 2X   birth,        rate b per individual
X → 0    death,        rate d per individual
```

Its first two moments, `Mean = E[X]` and `Variance = Var[X]`, close exactly:

```
dMean/dt     = s + (b − d)·Mean
dVariance/dt = s + (b + d)·Mean + 2·(b − d)·Variance
```

`moments.bngl` encodes precisely these two equations: its two ODE **states are the
mean and the variance**. The committed `moments.exp` holds both trajectories — for the
true `(s, b, d) = (1.0, 0.4, 0.8)` the process is subcritical (`b < d`), so the mean
relaxes to `−s/(b−d) = 2.5` and the variance to `5.0`.

## Fitting both moments

```
experiment: timecourse, data: moments.exp   # two columns: Obs_Mean, Obs_Variance

uniform_var = s           0.1   5.0
uniform_var = birth_rate  0.05  2.0
uniform_var = death_rate  0.05  3.0
```

There is nothing special in the config — the moments are ordinary observables, scored
column by column like any other. What makes the lesson is **which** observables they
are, and that fitting them **jointly** recovers all three rates:

```bash
pybnf -c moment_fit.conf      # recovers s ≈ 1.0, b ≈ 0.4, d ≈ 0.8
```

## Why you need *both* moments

The mean equation depends only on `s` and the **net** rate `b − d`. So the mean curve
alone pins `s` and `b − d`, but it **cannot separate birth from death**: raise `b` and
`d` together, keep `b − d` fixed, and the mean is identical. (A mean-only
profile-likelihood analysis shows exactly this — `birth_rate` comes back structurally
non-identifiable.)

The **variance** breaks the tie: it depends on the **sum** `b + d`. With the net
difference from the mean and the sum from the variance, all three rates are
identified — which is why the joint fit above recovers `b` and `d` separately, and a
mean-only fit could not. Fitting a higher moment is what turns a partially-identifiable
model into a fully-identifiable one.
